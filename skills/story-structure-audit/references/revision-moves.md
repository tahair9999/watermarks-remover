# Revision moves

Structural fixes, organized by the dimension they act on. The examples use invented
stories — substitute the actual text under audit.

The test every recommendation has to pass: **could the author apply this by editing
sentences?** If yes, it is a prose note wearing a structural costume. Real structural
revision changes what happens, in what order, or what the reader knows when.

Recommend two or three moves, not twelve. An audit that lists every possible change
transfers the editorial judgment back to the author, which is the part they wanted
help with.

---

## Thematic explicitness and narratorial commentary

The most common single fix, and usually the cheapest: the draft has already earned
its meaning and then says it anyway.

**Move: cut the gloss, end on the gesture.** Find the last place the story states
what it was about. Usually it is the final paragraph, sometimes a line of dialogue
where a character summarizes. Delete it and end on the concrete action immediately
preceding.

> Weak: "Make the theme less heavy-handed."
> Strong: "Cut the final two paragraphs where Mara reflects on what the summer taught
> her. End on her putting the unopened letter back in the drawer. Everything those
> paragraphs say is already in that action."

**Move: give the theme to a character who is wrong about it.** If the story needs the
idea voiced, let someone voice it self-servingly, so the reader has to weigh it
rather than receive it.

**Move: strip the narrator's editorial layer.** Where the narration steps out to
judge, cut to the character's own limited read of the same moment. The judgment
becomes the reader's job.

## Agency in resolution

**Move: let the decision fail to settle things.** Keep the protagonist's choice, but
have it not be what resolves the situation — or have it resolve the situation and
cost something the story refuses to call worth it.

> Strong: "Devi still confronts her brother in the last scene, but the inheritance
> was already signed over a week earlier. Her choice arrives too late to matter, and
> the story is about her discovering that, not about her deciding."

**Move: hand the ending to a minor character or to circumstance.** Ask what the story
would be if the resolution came from the neighbour, the weather, a bureaucratic
deadline. Often the answer is a better story, because the protagonist has to absorb
an outcome rather than author it.

## Plot track count and ending tidiness

**Move: promote something already in the draft to a live subplot.** Most drafts
contain an abandoned thread — a character mentioned twice, a job, an illness. Give it
two more scenes and let it stay unresolved.

**Move: leave one thread open on purpose, and know which.** Not vagueness — a
specific question the story deliberately declines to answer, which the reader can
feel is deliberate.

**Move: end one scene earlier.** Cut the denouement. If the story resolves in scene
fourteen and scenes fifteen and sixteen settle everyone's future, the story ends at
fourteen.

## Protagonist moral position

**Move: make the protagonist's gain someone else's loss, and don't square it.** The
gap here is not likability — it is whether the story lets its protagonist be
implicated. Find the moment the protagonist gets what they want and ask who paid.
Then resist the scene that absolves them.

**Move: remove the justification.** If the protagonist does something questionable
and the draft explains why it was understandable, cut the explanation. Let the act
stand.

## Event escalation

**Move: add a lull, or a false peak.** Flat and evenly-stepped curves both come from
every scene serving the next rung up. Put in a scene that goes nowhere in plot terms
but changes how the reader holds the characters — or a crisis that resolves early and
turns out not to have been the crisis.

**Move: vary scene granularity.** Render one small moment at length; summarize
something large in a sentence. Even intensity often comes from even *resolution*.

## Chronological discontinuity

Handle with care. Discontinuity in published fiction is usually a consequence of a
story needing two time frames, not an ornament added for texture. Inserting a
flashback because a scorecard said so produces a worse story that scores better,
which is the failure mode this whole file exists to avoid.

**Move: find the story's second time frame, if it has one.** Ask whether there is an
earlier event the present action is actually about. If so, the story may want to
carry both, interleaved. If not, leave the chronology alone and say so in the audit.

**Move: open later and recover the setup in fragments.** Start at the point of
commitment, and let the reader assemble the preceding circumstances from what
characters say and withhold.

## Recontextualization depth

**Move: plant the reveal's evidence early, read innocently.** Put the detail in
scene two where it means something ordinary. When the reveal lands, it should change
what scene two was, not merely answer a question.

**Move: reveal something about a *past* scene rather than about a fact.** The
difference between "the letter was from her mother" and learning that the argument in
chapter one was staged for her benefit.

## Emotion channel

The widest measured gap, and the easiest to overcorrect. The fix is not to eliminate
physical description — it is to stop routing *every* feeling through the body.

**Move: name the emotion outright, where the character would.** Published fiction
does this far more than workshop advice allows. If she is humiliated, the narration
can say she was humiliated.

**Move: convey emotion through choice instead of sensation.** What someone does when
frightened — what they pick up, who they call, what they decide not to mention —
carries more than their heart rate.

**Move: audit the smell imagery specifically.** The paper found it markedly
overrepresented. Count the instances; keep the one that earns its place.

## Intertextual strategy

**Move: name the thing.** Where the draft gestures at a book, a song, a painting
without naming it, name it. Specificity situates the story in a real world and
carries characterization — *which* book someone loves says something.

## Perspective and frame

**Move: let the narration acknowledge it is a telling.** One aside, one admission
that the narrator does not know something, one direct address. Sealed, consistent
narration is the machine-typical default; published fiction leaks.

---

## When the honest recommendation is "change nothing"

Sometimes a story scores AI-leaning across the board and is fine: a tidy, sincere,
linear story that states its theme is a legitimate and ancient form. Say that. An
audit that always finds three things to fix is not diagnosing, it is performing.

The pattern that genuinely warrants concern is not any single score but *defaults*
— a draft where every structural choice went the conventional way, which usually
means no choice was made at all. That is worth naming, and it is a craft observation
rather than a detection one.
